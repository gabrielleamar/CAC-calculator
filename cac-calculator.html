    Statins slightly increase the risk of progression from prediabetes to type 2 diabetes, especially with high-intensity therapy (~0.3 excess cases per 100 patient-years).

    Key Point: The cardiovascular benefit consistently outweighs this risk. New-onset diabetes is NOT a reason to avoid or discontinue statin therapy.

    Statin-Intolerant or Refusing Patients

    Before abandoning statins: Trial 2–3 different statins (including lowest dose or alternate-day dosing with long half-life statin like rosuvastatin). Most patients with side effects can tolerate rechallenge.

    Ezetimibe: First-line when <25% additional LDL-C lowering needed (oral, generic, low cost).

    • Did not significantly reduce stroke, CV death, or all-cause mortality individually

    What is Coronary Artery Calcium (CAC)?

    Understanding CAC scoring in plain language.

    Imagine your heart's arteries as highways that carry life-giving blood to your heart muscle. Over time, cholesterol and other substances can build up along these highways, forming what we call atherosclerotic plaque. As this plaque ages and evolves, it can develop calcium deposits—think of them like little "scars" or "fossils" within the artery walls.

    Coronary Artery Calcium (CAC) is essentially a window into this process. When we measure CAC, we're detecting and quantifying these calcium deposits, which tell us how much plaque has built up in your coronary arteries over the years. It's like taking a snapshot of your heart's "wear and tear" from atherosclerosis.

    These calcium deposits start tiny—microscopic specks smaller than a human hair—and can grow into larger, sheet-like deposits over time. Most of this calcification happens in the inner lining of your arteries, where plaque tends to accumulate.

    How Do We Measure CAC?

    The CAC scan is quick, painless, and remarkably simple. You lie on a table, hold your breath for just a few seconds, and a CT scanner takes images of your heart. No needles, no fasting, no contrast dye needed—it's that straightforward.

    The computer then analyzes these images using something called the Agatston method. Here's how it works: the scanner looks for areas that appear brighter than a certain threshold (130 Hounsfield units, if you're curious about the technical details). It measures both the size of these bright areas and how dense they are, then combines this information into a single number—your CAC score.

    The radiation exposure is minimal—about the same as a mammogram and far less than a typical CT scan. So you get valuable information with very little risk.

    What Does Your CAC Score Mean?

    Your CAC score falls into one of these categories, each telling a different story about your heart health:

    CAC Score What It Means The Story It Tells
    0 No detectable calcium Great news! Your arteries appear clear of calcified plaque. Your near-term risk of heart events is very low.
    1–99 Mild calcification Some plaque is present, but it's early and limited. This is your wake-up call to take heart health seriously.
    100–299 Moderate calcification Significant plaque has built up over time. Your heart is sending a clear message that preventive action is needed.
    300–999 Severe calcification Extensive plaque is present. This is serious, but knowing means you can take action to protect your heart.
    ≥1000 Very severe calcification Widespread plaque throughout your coronary arteries. Aggressive prevention and treatment are essential.

    Why CAC Matters

    CAC is like a crystal ball for your heart. It's incredibly good at finding plaque—about 91% sensitive for detecting significant blockages. However, it's not perfect at telling us how severe that blockage might be, which is why we always interpret CAC scores in the context of your overall health picture.

    Age matters too. Younger people tend to have more "soft" plaque that hasn't yet calcified, while older adults typically have more calcified plaque. This means a CAC score of 50 means something different for a 45-year-old versus a 70-year-old.

    Think of CAC as measuring the extent of atherosclerosis—how much plaque is there—rather than how dangerous that plaque might be right now. It's a powerful tool for risk stratification, helping us see where you stand compared to others your age.

    What About "Incidental" CAC?

    Sometimes we find CAC by accident. You might get a chest CT for lung cancer screening or another reason, and the radiologist notices calcium in your heart arteries. This is called "incidental CAC," and it's actually valuable information!

    Even though these scans aren't specifically designed for CAC scoring, doctors can still estimate your calcium burden. Modern technology even uses artificial intelligence to automatically detect and quantify CAC on routine chest CTs—turning an unexpected finding into actionable insight about your heart health.

    The Science Behind CAC:

    CAC develops alongside atherosclerotic plaque as smooth muscle cells within the plaque undergo apoptosis—essentially, programmed cell death. The earliest calcification appears as microscopic deposits within the arterial wall's inner lining. Over time, these deposits cluster deeper within the plaque's lipid core, near the artery's internal elastic membrane.

    Interestingly, the pattern and extent of calcification don't directly correlate with how large the plaque is or how active it might be. This means CAC tells us about plaque burden rather than immediate danger.

    Technical Details:

    The scan uses 2.5–3.0 mm slice thickness and identifies calcified areas at least 1 mm² in size with CT density exceeding 130 Hounsfield units. The Agatston method then applies a density weighting factor—areas with higher density get multiplied by larger numbers. Anatomic features like which vessels are involved and whether calcification is localized or widespread add additional predictive value.

    Here's something fascinating: higher calcified lesion density may actually be associated with lower cardiovascular risk. This seems counterintuitive, but it likely reflects greater plaque stability—denser, more calcified plaques may be less likely to rupture and cause heart attacks.

    Why CAC Scoring Is So Patient-Friendly:

    Unlike many cardiac tests, CAC scoring requires no special preparation. No fasting, no contrast dye, no medications beforehand. The entire scan takes just 3–5 seconds while you hold your breath. Modern scanners can even complete the scan with less than 0.4 mSv of radiation—truly minimal exposure for maximum information.

    The Science Behind CAC Scoring and Statin Therapy

    What Happens When Your Calcium Score Is Zero?

    A groundbreaking study followed over 13,000 adults for nearly a decade, and the findings were remarkable. People with a calcium score of zero derived essentially no benefit from statin therapy—the numbers showed no meaningful difference in heart events between those taking statins and those who weren't.

    But here's the fascinating part: for people with any detectable calcium, statins made a real difference. The more calcium present, the greater the benefit. To put this in perspective, doctors would need to treat 549 people with a zero calcium score for five years to prevent just one heart event. For people with high calcium scores, that number drops to just 24.

    Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30409567/

    The MESA Study: What It Taught Us About Risk

    The Multiethnic Study of Atherosclerosis (MESA) provided crucial insights. Even among patients with multiple risk factors for heart disease, those with a calcium score of zero had event rates so low that treatment wasn't clearly beneficial. This finding held true across nearly all risk-enhancing factors.

    The conclusion was clear: a zero calcium score is a powerful "de-risker"—it tells us that despite other risk factors, your near-term risk of heart events is genuinely low. However, this doesn't apply to everyone. People with familial hypercholesterolemia, very high cholesterol, diabetes, smoking, or strong family history of heart disease still need treatment regardless of their calcium score.

    What Do Experts Around the World Say?

    When researchers reviewed guidelines from major heart organizations across the globe—including the American College of Cardiology, European Society of Cardiology, Canadian Cardiovascular Society, and others—they found remarkable agreement. Most societies endorse withholding statin therapy when calcium scores are zero, provided patients don't have other high-risk conditions.

    While there's some variation in exactly when to recommend statins at higher calcium scores, the core principle is consistent: a zero calcium score provides meaningful reassurance about near-term heart risk.

    Why Guidelines Sometimes Disagree

    Decision-making isn't always straightforward. Different expert organizations have slightly different approaches to calcium scoring, which can make things confusing:

    American and Canadian guidelines reserve calcium scoring for younger adults who have specific risk factors. European guidelines have been more cautious, noting concerns about cost, radiation exposure, and limited benefit in younger or lower-risk individuals. British guidelines use calcium scoring as a "gatekeeper" test for younger patients with intermediate risk who have symptoms.

    The bottom line? The "right" answer can depend on which guideline framework you're following. This calculator follows the American College of Cardiology and American Heart Association approach, which is widely used in the United States.

    The Gray Zone: When Decisions Are Hardest

    Where Calcium Testing Shines

    Calcium testing is most valuable precisely when clinical decision-making is most uncertain—patients at borderline-to-intermediate risk (roughly 5% to 20% ten-year risk of heart disease) where the benefit of statin therapy isn't clear-cut.

    A review in the Journal of the American Medical Association Cardiology emphasized that calcium testing should be used selectively in these patients. It's not for everyone—it's most helpful after standard risk assessment and consideration of other risk factors still leave the treatment decision unclear. Think of it as a tiebreaker when the evidence points in different directions.

    The Evidence Behind Nonstatin Alternatives

    What If You Can't Take Statins?

    For patients who truly cannot tolerate statins or refuse them, research has identified effective alternatives. The CLEAR Outcomes trial studied nearly 14,000 patients who couldn't take statins and found that bempedoic acid reduced major heart events by 13% over about three and a half years. The benefit was comparable to what we see with statins for each unit of cholesterol lowered.

    However, it's important to note that in primary prevention (people without established heart disease), only statins have consistently shown benefits on both heart events and mortality. Other medications like ezetimibe and PCSK9 inhibitors have primarily shown benefit in high-risk patients who already have heart disease.
